If a VPN (Virtual Private Network) is a kind of foundational, background thing that's supposed to cover the entire spectrum, then a proxy is a highly specialized tool. You use a proxy *for something specific*. VPNs always have dirty IP addresses; the whole world knows you're connecting through a VPN, and there's nothing you can do about it. They can't track where you're *really* coming from, but the fact that you're coming from a VPN is visible—their IPs are burned, listed everywhere in all sorts of databases.
Whereas SOCKS IPs need to be clean. That's their entire point, and that's what people pay money for. But they use them for all sorts of shenanigans. At a minimum, for registering multiple accounts; at a maximum, for stealing money. So, generally speaking, SOCKS are used for this kind of stuff. Plus, they are very slow, I mean the speed. If a proxy gives you 2-3 megabits per second, that's like, whoa, amazing! It's excellent, it's a turbo sock! You can't live with that speed; not a single call will go through. Therefore, SOCKS are strictly for specific tasks, while a VPN is the base layer.But why are SOCKS proxies so slow? They aren't slow for no reason; someone is sourcing them. There are whole companies that buy up pools of addresses from small-time ISPs in the backwaters of Europe or America. Because with the big providers, everything is under control as usual, but some local ISP in Germany or Poland can easily sell you a subnet, and no one will even notice. And these IPs can be distributed as SOCKS proxies tied to locations. If you connect with an IP from Miami, you're allocated a server that's literally sticking out of an apartment in Florida. And most people don't know that their router is sharing out a proxy service for Bitcoin to some shady service.Sometimes it's some home basement farm, for example, on Raspberry Pis, or from hundreds of cheap VPSs running thousands of proxies. And if they give you 2 Mbps, that's still good, but sometimes it's 512 kbit, like back in 2007. Why? Because if they provided a normal bandwidth channel, it would be devoured by bots, DDoSers, or those who "move money." And if we take classic SOCKS, for example, its main characteristic is that it operates at the application level. This guarantees complete invisibility not for the entire device, but only and exclusively for a specific application. Only on random machines—PCs, cheap VPSs, sometimes even smartphones.That's the difference. If a VPN creates an encrypted tunnel for *all* network traffic of the operating system, redirecting it through a remote server, then SOCKS works differently. It functions as a proxy protocol that redirects network packets *only from specific applications* configured to work with it. It's a pinpoint replacement of the IP address with a high level of trust in that address. This trust exists because, for example, a website's protection sees an authentic session from some small town, from a private network, and doesn't trigger additional checks. In network systems, they are identified as ordinary user connections from individual ISPs, and accordingly, their cleanliness is critically important.The low bandwidth, however, is a direct consequence of the very architecture of how they are obtained. Because all these addresses that belong to SOCKS proxies are not hosted on servers in daaaaaaata centers that are super high-performance. Often, these are pools (ranges of SOCKS IPs) leased from small providers, and physically the servers might be deployed on low-power equipment. Because if a "residential" IP address suddenly starts showing gigabit traffic typical only for data centers—for something large-scale—it would instantly raise a red flag with all security systems.

Anti-fraud systems of all giants like Google or banks track such inconsistencies. But they monitor their *own* addresses, whereas some local ISP in Romania or the US might sell a subnet, and no one will notice. These IPs are then distributed as SOCKS—"Here's an IP from Miami, here's one from Berlin." An IP address that is officially "residential" but behaves like a "server" will be quickly detected and, again, blacklisted. So, you compromise yourself. And here we return to the fact that your new IP will be added to a list used for attacks, parsing, bots, etc.What's needed is a pinpoint replacement of the IP address with a high level of trust in that address, not comprehensive protection or anonymization of the entire network connection. SOCKS is a tool for specific scenarios, and it's not profitable at all for the SOCKS provider to provide a large channel. The business model itself is built on slicing up one channel from a small provider into hundreds of slow proxies and selling them individually.

This isn't always due to a bad proxy; sometimes it's because of the ping parameter. Ping is the time it takes for a signal to travel from one place to another. If you're standing on the roof of a skyscraper and your friend is on the roof of the skyscraper opposite, the time between you shouting "on the count of 3" and when he hears you—that interval is the ping. It's the time it takes for your data packet to travel from your PC to the server, for example to the proxy server or a website, and back. It's measured in milliseconds. Low ping (from 10-15ms) means the connection is fast, and you'll get the response "Let's go!" almost instantly. High ping (200ms and above) means the response takes a long time (video freezing, page loading slowly, game lagging, your friend thinking...), constant buffering and low quality. Every signal travels thousands of kilometers over physical paths, cables, routers, and this journey takes time. The farther the proxy server is from you, the longer the data has to travel back and forth. For tasks with frequent requests, like parsing or multi-accounting, this will multiply the total working time. If you need a proxy but don't want to deal with high ping, you should choose a proxy from a region closest to you. The most effective way to reduce your ping is to choose a proxy server that is geographically close to you. A network is a number 'n' of devices (minimum 2), connected to each other in such a way that they can exchange information. And everything connected to the router or by cable - it's all united into a local network. And the router is a gateway, meaning a device that allows every client (phone, robot vacuum, PC, etc.) to enjoy the internet. And when we connect something to the router, the router gives that device its own local IP address, which is why only devices within your local network can access your device. And even if a neighbor wants to connect to your PC, they won't be able to do it because they don't have access to your local network, because they themselves have a device with the same kind of local address. And the router itself gets access to the network through its provider.If we have Router1 and Router2 and they are clients (connected by fiber optic cable or twisted pair) of the same provider's router, then this will be the provider's local network. And this kind of nested matryoshka of networks, so that all clients can access the internet, progresses all the way to the main gateway which has a public IP address - this is the provider's Data Center. But the public address belongs only to the provider, not to us, and why is it needed? So that when we go online we can get a response from the server.If we need to go to YouTube, we make a DNS query to find out where YouTube is located, because it needs to know the IP address of the target server. Your PC expresses an unbearable desire to deliver an HTTP request to the IP server where YouTube is located. The router takes this request and checks if it has direct access to the network where the desired IP address is located, and logically realizing that it doesn't have such access, it passes this request further, to its own gateway, and so the request reaches the main gateway, which has that very public IP.

The gateway sends the request further to the internet, to a network consisting of public IP addresses, and as the return address, where the response needs to be sent, it specifies its own public IP, and assigns a specific port on itself to the network session, and this technology is called NAT or Network Address Translation, and thanks to it, an embedded nesting doll of networks is formed, and because of it, it's impossible from the outside to reach the local IP addresses. When the request reaches the YouTube server, the server will send the response to the public address of your provider, since it doesn't know your device's IP address, because it's impossible to access it from the outside, and then the routers along the local chain will pass it to your device. Anyone outside your small local network cannot send packets to your machine without a request that was sent from your device to the outside, meaning I can send information to the internet, but the internet can only send information to you in response to your request. I myself can initiate connections outward. The internet is like a vampire, which can only enter after being invited???. And that is precisely why you can't just be hacked remotely, simply through your internet connection, because your PC needs to request this connection itself first. And that is precisely why the main method of attacks behind NAT is malicious software, whose task boils down to opening a path for the attacker by initiating a connection to their proxy server.

VPN allows you to create your own local network between devices that are not physically in the same local network, meaning they are not connected by wires or waves. When we connect to a VPN server, for example in Germany, a separate local network is created between your PC and that server in Germany where the server and the PC can see each other and initiate connections in both directions. That is, we can reach out to that server in Germany, and that server can reach out to you. And this network is isolated using encryption, meaning along the path from your device to the server, reading this data is impossible, therefore your provider cannot know what you are requesting on the network, it only sees that your device is sending encrypted data to some server and routes it to that server or not, if your VPN protocol is blocked or if your VPN address is, for example, on your provider's blacklist and it simply blocks the traffic, but that's about internet censorship and those are specifics. So, it's the VPN server that goes to the internet on your behalf, not your provider's gateway, which is why your public address changes, meaning the VPN server becomes the new gateway for your machine. Well, and what about other users of your VPN, would they also end up in my local network if they were connected by a wire?? And yes and no, it all depends on how the VPN is configured. It can be configured for client isolation, and then only the server and the client can see each other, but client-to-client connection is impossible, or they can all see each other, and then I and all participants can directly access your device over the network and carry out various attacks. VPN was not invented so that you could bypass blocks, but to create a distributed network structure, for example for business needs; it's simply that VPN allows hiding the content of the network traffic (from the provider), which is obviously understood what it's used for. Here we can attach the concept of free VPN, because if I'm not paying for the product, then I am the product. And this rule always works. Infrastructure, servers, configuration, traffic - all that costs a lot, and who could be so kind??
VPN changes your risks. If I don't use a VPN, the provider interferes in which sites I can visit, because it sees in the open the IP addresses and domains the device wants to connect to. Only the data itself that I transmit to these sites, which they transmit to me, is hidden from it. And if I use a VPN, there is a risk that from the server side or from the virtual network side, someone could hack you. And if I set up my personal VPN on my own server and share configs with friends or relatives, because if I haven't configured client isolation on the network, then if the config falls into the wrong hands, or people who cannot set up their own VPN, there is always a risk that someone will misuse it, bring in a virus, and someone might gain a direct communication channel with all participants of my virtual network, and also the risk of intercepting data that is transmitted in the open, but that risk exists without VPN too, it's just a question of who can intercept it.
VPN creates a virtual network adapter in my system and can reconfigure my device's routing in such a way that the VPN server becomes my gateway to the internet. This means all the device's network traffic can go through the VPN, and then nothing needs to be configured separately in programs like a browser. The IP will change everywhere. And using a VPN, it's convenient to create chains like Double VPN, Triple VPN, and so on...
That is, when I connect to an entry server, the traffic is redirected to an exit server and only then goes to the internet. This is used against timing attacks, correlating logs from the provider and the target website. Here, the connection times and IP addresses are correlated if there is access to both the provider's logs and the website logs. But if the entry and exit IP addresses are different, it becomes difficult to figure out.

PROXY
The implementation method here differs from VPN. Since VPN creates a network and encrypted data channels within that network, a proxy simply forwards the user's packets and relays the responses received from the target server. A proxy does not create virtual adapters or a network, and proxy protocols do not have their own encryption. To use a proxy, you need to assign it to the program that will use it to access the internet. This is done in the settings of the program itself and also externally, at the system level, using tools like Proxifier (Win, Mac, Android) and proxychains (Linux). Even though the proxy lacks encryption, this means the server consumes fewer resources, the client does too, and speed is higher. If a person is doing something large-scale, like SMM tasks, arbitrage, multi-accounting, which loads resources, then proxy is what's needed. And you can use many proxies in parallel. Because a proxy is configured inside the program and not externally at the system level like a VPN, the software itself can decide how to juggle hundreds of proxy configs. And I can have a program running that logs into 100 accounts, and for each one, it uses its own proxy. The risks – if the target website starts flooding you with CAPTCHAs or doesn't allow login because the IP address is heavily spammed... Because if the proxy says: "Honey, I'm the first one using this!" that's a lie. And what was done with this proxy and my IP address in the past by previous users is shrouded in the darkness of mystery.

PROXY
способ реализации тут отличаеться от VPN,поскольку vpn создает сеть и зашифрованные каналы данных внутри этой сети,а прокси просто пересылает дальше пакеты пользователя и пересылает ответы полученные от целевого сервера. Прокси не создает ни виртуальных адаптеров,ни сети и протоколы проксирования не имеют собственного шифрования и чтобы использовать прокси его нужно назначить программе которая будет его использовать для выхода в интернет и это делаеться в настройках самой программы  и так же извне, на системном уровне используя инструменты как Proxifier(Win,Mac,Android) и proxychains(Lixnux).Пускай в прокси нет шифрования,но это значит что сервер расходует меньше ресурсов,клиент тоже,выше скорость.если человек решает чтото масштабное,например smm задачи,арбитраж,мультиаккаунтингом,что загружает ресурсы,то прокси - это то что нужно. и можно использовать много прокси паралельно.потому что прокси настраиваеться внутри программы а не извне на системном уровне как vpn,то софт сам может решать как жанглировать сотнями прокси конфигов,  и у меня может быть запущена прога которая заходит в 100 аккаунтов и для каждого он использует свой прокси. риски- если целевой сайт будет заваливать тебя капчей или не даст залогинится если ip адрес сильно заспамлен,потому что если прокси говорит,что: "Милый,тф у меня первый!" то это ложь и что с этим прокси и моим ip адресов делали в прошлом предыдущие  пользователи-покрыто мраком тайн.
